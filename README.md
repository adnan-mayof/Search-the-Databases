# Step 5 — Search the Databases

[← Previous Step: Test and Refine the Search Strategy](https://github.com/adnan-mayof/Test-and-Refine-the-Search-Strategy/blob/main/README.md/)

## 🧭 Maya Continues Her Evidence Synthesis Journey

Maya has now:

* Developed her research question.
* Identified her PICO/PICOS concepts.
* Developed keywords and synonyms.
* Adapted her search strategy for different databases.
* Tested her search strategy against known relevant studies.
* Refined and documented her search strategy.

Her mentor tells her that she is now ready to conduct the **final searches**.

> **Maya:** “So now I just search PubMed and Scopus?”

> **Mentor:** “Yes—but remember, the goal is not simply to search two databases. You need to search **all databases that are appropriate for your research question**.”

Maya opens her research plan.

She has identified databases that may contain literature about AI, education, learning, and technology.

She is ready to begin.

---

# 1. Select the Databases

Maya first asks an important question:

> **Maya:** “How do I know which databases I should search?”

Her mentor explains:

> **Mentor:** “The databases should be selected based on your research question and the types of evidence you need to find.”

For Maya's question about AI-powered learning technologies and student learning outcomes, relevant databases may include databases covering:

* Education
* Psychology
* Technology
* Computer science
* Health or biomedical research, when relevant

For example, Maya might consider:

| Database           | Why it may be useful                      |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| **ERIC**           | Education research                        |
| **PsycINFO**       | Psychology, learning, cognition, behavior |
| **Scopus**         | Broad multidisciplinary coverage          |
| **Web of Science** | Multidisciplinary scholarly literature    |
| **PubMed**         | Biomedical and health-related literature  |
| **IEEE Xplore**    | Computing, engineering, and technology    |

The exact databases depend on the scope of the review.

---

# 2. Why Search Multiple Databases?

Maya asks:

> **Maya:** “Why can't I just use Scopus? It already has a lot of studies.”

Her mentor explains:

> **Mentor:** “Because no single database contains everything.”

Different databases have different:

* Coverage
* Journals
* Conferences
* Subject areas
* Indexing systems
* Publication types
* Historical coverage

A study that appears in one database may not appear in another.

Therefore, searching multiple appropriate databases can help Maya identify a more comprehensive set of potentially relevant studies.

---

# 3. Use the Final Search Strategy

Maya now takes the search strategies she finalized in Step 4.

She does **not** start changing her search randomly.

Instead, she uses the documented final version for each database.

For example, her PubMed strategy may include:

```text
("Students"[MeSH Terms]
OR student*[Title/Abstract]
OR learner*[Title/Abstract]
OR undergraduate*[Title/Abstract]
OR universit*[Title/Abstract])
AND
("Artificial Intelligence"[MeSH Terms]
OR "artificial intelligence"[Title/Abstract]
OR "generative artificial intelligence"[Title/Abstract]
OR "generative AI"[Title/Abstract]
OR GenAI[Title/Abstract]
OR chatbot*[Title/Abstract]
OR "intelligent tutoring system*"[Title/Abstract]
OR "adaptive learning"[Title/Abstract])
AND
("learning outcome*"[Title/Abstract]
OR achievement[Title/Abstract]
OR performance[Title/Abstract]
OR learning[Title/Abstract]
OR knowledge[Title/Abstract]
OR skill*[Title/Abstract])
```

Her Scopus strategy uses the appropriate Scopus syntax.

The important point is that these searches represent the **same research concepts**, even though their database-specific syntax differs.

---

# 4. Search Each Database Separately

Maya begins with PubMed.

She runs the final search and records the number of results.

Then she moves to Scopus.

She runs the adapted Scopus strategy and records the number of results.

She continues with each database included in her protocol.

Her mentor reminds her:

> **Mentor:** “Do not combine the databases while searching. First, keep each database's results identifiable.”

Maya understands why.

If she keeps the results separate, she can later determine:

* Which database produced each record
* How many records came from each database
* Which database produced duplicates
* Whether a particular database contributed unique records

---

# 5. Record the Search Date

Maya notices something important.

Search results can change over time.

New studies can be added to databases.

> **Maya:** “So I need to record when I searched?”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly.”

For each database, Maya records the **search date**.

For example:

```text
Database: PubMed
Search date: September XX, 2026
Records retrieved: XXXX
```

She does the same for every database.

The search date becomes part of the audit trail for the review.

---

# 6. Save the Complete Search Strategy

Maya copies the complete search strategy into her research repository.

She does not record only:

> “Searched PubMed for AI and students.”

That would not be enough.

Instead, she saves the **complete search string** exactly as it was run.

For example:

```text
search/
├── search-terms.md
├── pubmed-search.md
├── scopus-search.md
├── eric-search.md
├── psycinfo-search.md
└── search-log.md
```

This allows another researcher to understand what Maya actually searched.

---

# 7. Record the Number of Results

After each search, Maya records how many records were retrieved.

For example:

| Database | Search date | Records retrieved |
| -------- | ----------- | ----------------: |
| PubMed   | XX/XX/2026  |             1,245 |
| Scopus   | XX/XX/2026  |             2,871 |
| ERIC     | XX/XX/2026  |             1,034 |
| PsycINFO | XX/XX/2026  |               987 |

These numbers are examples only.

Maya will use the **actual numbers from her searches** in her review.

---

# 8. Do Not Screen the Studies Yet

Maya sees thousands of records.

> **Maya:** “Should I start reading the titles now?”

Her mentor stops her.

> **Mentor:** “Not yet. First, finish collecting the search results from all of your selected databases.”

Maya realizes that searching and screening are different stages.

At this stage, her goal is to **identify and collect potentially relevant records**, not decide which studies should ultimately be included.

The screening process comes later.

---

# 9. Do Not Delete Duplicates Manually During the Search

Maya notices that the same article appears in several databases.

> **Maya:** “I found the same study in PubMed and Scopus. Should I delete one now?”

> **Mentor:** “No. Keep the records from each database. We will manage duplicates systematically after the searches are complete.”

This is important because Maya needs to preserve the original search results and database sources.

Later, she can combine the results and remove duplicates using a reference-management or screening tool.

That will be the next stage of the workflow.

---

# 10. Consider Search Limits Carefully

Maya notices that databases offer filters such as:

* Publication date
* Language
* Study type
* Age
* Document type

She asks:

> **Maya:** “Should I use all these filters to make the search smaller?”

Her mentor responds:

> **Mentor:** “Only when they are justified by your eligibility criteria and review protocol.”

A filter should not be added simply because it makes the search easier to manage.

For example, if Maya's eligibility criteria require studies published from a particular year onward, a publication-date limit may be appropriate.

But if she excludes studies merely because the search results are too large, she may unintentionally remove relevant evidence.

The key principle is:

> **Search limits should be justified by the review criteria, not by convenience.**

---

# 11. What If the Search Produces Too Many Results?

Maya searches one database and gets a very large number of records.

> **Maya:** “This is a lot of records. Should I immediately make the search narrower?”

Her mentor says:

> **Mentor:** “Not automatically.”

Maya should first consider:

* Are the results actually relevant to the concepts?
* Is one search term producing many irrelevant records?
* Is the outcome concept too broad?
* Is the population concept too broad?
* Did she accidentally use an overly general term?
* Are database-specific fields being used correctly?
* Is a justified search refinement needed?

If the search needs refinement, Maya should document the change and rerun the search.

This connects back to **Step 4 — Test and Refine the Search Strategy**.

---

# 12. What If the Search Produces Too Few Results?

The opposite problem can also happen.

Maya searches a database and receives very few results.

Her mentor reminds her:

> **Mentor:** “A small number of results is not automatically a problem. But you should investigate whether your search is too narrow.”

Maya can examine:

* Whether important synonyms are missing
* Whether the search fields are appropriate
* Whether phrase searching is too restrictive
* Whether the database uses different terminology
* Whether the concepts were translated correctly into that database's syntax
* Whether a search limit is unnecessarily restricting retrieval

Again, the goal is not simply to maximize or minimize the number of records.

The goal is to create a search that is **appropriate for the research question**.

---

# 13. Keep an Audit Trail

Maya now understands that searching is not something she should do informally.

She keeps a record of what happened.

Her search log might look like this:

```text
Database: Scopus

Search date: XX/XX/2026

Final search strategy:
[complete search string]

Fields searched:
TITLE-ABS-KEY

Limits:
[document any limits used]

Records retrieved:
XXXX

Notes:
Search strategy adapted from the core PICO concepts.
```

If she makes a change, she records it.

For example:

```text
Change:
Added "intelligent tutoring system*" to intervention terms.

Reason:
Known relevant studies used this terminology.

Date:
XX/XX/2026
```

This creates a transparent record of the search process.

---

# 14. PRISMA 2020 and Reporting the Search

Maya remembers what she learned in Step 4.

PRISMA 2020 Item 7 concerns reporting the search strategy.

The search process should be documented clearly enough that readers can understand how the evidence was identified.

Maya therefore saves:

* Database names
* Search dates
* Complete search strategies
* Search fields
* Controlled vocabulary when applicable
* Keywords and synonyms
* Limits and filters
* Number of records retrieved
* Important adaptations or changes

This documentation will also help her later when preparing the PRISMA flow diagram.

---

After several hours, Maya finishes.

She has searched all of her selected databases.

Her repository now contains:

```text
search/
├── search-terms.md
├── pubmed-search.md
├── scopus-search.md
├── eric-search.md
├── psycinfo-search.md
└── search-log.md
```

She has also recorded the number of records retrieved from each database.

Maya looks at the results.

> **Maya:** “There are thousands of records!”

> **Mentor:** “That's okay. Remember, we haven't decided which studies belong in the review yet.”

> **Maya:** “So what happens next?”

> **Mentor:** “Before we move on to managing the records, we need to formally document what we plan to do in the review.”

> **Maya:** “You mean the protocol?”

> **Mentor:** “Exactly. Now that you have defined the research question and search strategy, it is time to **develop and register the review protocol**.”

Maya opens a new document.

> **Maya:** “So the protocol will document how I plan to conduct the rest of the review?”

> **Mentor:** “Yes. It makes your methods explicit before you continue with screening, data extraction, and synthesis.”

Maya is ready for the next stage.

---

# ⭐ Important Principle

> **Search all appropriate databases using the finalized, database-adapted search strategies; record exactly what was searched, when it was searched, and how many records were retrieved.**

The workflow is:

```text
Select Appropriate Databases
          ↓
Use Final Database-Specific Strategies
          ↓
Run Each Search
          ↓
Record Search Date
          ↓
Save Complete Search String
          ↓
Record Number of Results
          ↓
Document Filters / Limits
          ↓
Preserve Database-Specific Results
          ↓
Document the Search Process
          ↓
Develop and Register the Protocol
```
